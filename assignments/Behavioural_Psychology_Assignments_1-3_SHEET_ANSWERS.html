My Go-To Coping Strategy

The one I rely on most is walking - around the campus block, usually at night, usually with a podcast or nothing at all. Learning the Unwritten Rules
Based on Socialization Simulation
Think of a real time you entered a new social setting (your first day of college, an internship, a new club, a new city). What unwritten "rules" of that group did you have to figure out, and how did you adapt while staying true to yourself?
The setting was my first week in the SRM student developer circle - the group of students who build tools and publish them publicly - and I arrived assuming the rules were the same as in class: do the work, ask questions, wait for the answer. The unwritten rules I had to work out were mostly about how, not what. Nobody announces a deadline; the deadline is the date someone casually mentions it and then acts as if it was always known. Praise is not given in a meeting - it is given by someone forwarding your work with one line above it, and if nobody forwards it, it may as well not have happened. The unwritten rule about disagreeing was the one I got wrong first: you do not push back on a design in the group channel, you push back in a message to the person, with a reason attached, and usually a smaller suggestion. Two people had been "corrected" in public and quietly left the group within a month. How I adapted while staying true to myself: I kept my style of writing things down and explaining the reasoning, because that was genuinely me - but I moved the explanation from the channel to the person, and I started asking privately first. The part I did not change was showing up with working code. That rule was the one that was written down, and it turned out to be the only one that mattered as much as the others. My mistake was assuming that because I was good at the work, I did not need the unwritten rules. The work got me in; the rules kept me there.
A Different Perspective
Based on Embracing Diversity
Describe a real conversation or friendship you've had with someone from a different cultural, linguistic, religious, or regional background than your own. What did you learn from them that you hadn't known before?
The conversation was with a teammate who grew up speaking a different language at home, and studied in a school system where the medium of instruction was different from the one I had known. What I learned that I had not known before was how much of an instruction is assumed to be shared. I had always thought of a teacher's phrasing as a detail on top of the actual content - you understand the concept and the wording does not matter. What I heard from my teammate was that in their schooling, a large part of what a question was asking had to be inferred, because the phrasing was not precise and precision was not assumed. Their strategy was to ask what the question wanted rather than to guess, and they found my way of working - treating the exact wording as the precise statement - unfamiliar. The second thing I took from it was about feedback. When I gave feedback quickly and directly, they took it as a verdict on their ability rather than on their work, because in their experience corrections were rarer and therefore heavier. I have since slowed down on corrections in writing and added the context first. Neither of us changed how we work - we just noticed that we were using the same words to mean different things, and that was the source of the friction.
My Code of Ethics
Based on Ethics & Moral Behaviour
Describe a real situation where you had to choose between doing what was easy and doing what you believed was right. What did you decide, what value guided that decision, and would you choose the same way again?
The situation: a junior on my team had pushed a part of a project with a bug, and the fix was faster if I quietly rewrote their part myself and presented it as mine, with no mention of where it came from. The easy thing was to do it that way - it protected the deadline and it protected the junior from embarrassment. What I believed was right: fix it with them, but do not hide who wrote what. So I sat with them for forty minutes, we found the actual bug (it was a state variable not being reset between runs, not a logic error at all), and we changed it together. It cost me about an hour of my own time that I would have spent elsewhere. The value that guided the decision was honesty about work, and more precisely a refusal to let someone else's effort be absorbed silently. I have been on the receiving end of that - work of mine presented as someone else's - and the version of me that existed before that happened is not someone I wanted to be. Would I choose the same way again? Yes, and not only because it is right. The practical reason is that hidden work does not stay hidden: the next bug in that code is mine to debug, and the next person who reads it learns that rewriting silently is normal. The ethical choice and the durable choice pointed the same way. What I would change is the process - I should have raised the "we do it together" rule with the team before the deadline, not during it.
